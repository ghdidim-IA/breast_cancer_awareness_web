Pink October

Raising awareness, understanding breast cancer, and promoting informed decisions about breast health.

Every October, people around the world come together to talk about breast cancer. Understanding the basics helps you notice changes sooner, ask better questions, and support the people in your life who are affected.

Why awareness matters

Breast cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide. Awareness helps people recognise changes, understand that screening exists, and seek care without fear or stigma. It also reminds us that access to information and treatment is still unequal between and within countries.

Understanding breast cancer

The breast contains lobules, which make milk, and ducts, which carry it to the nipple. These are surrounded by fatty and connective tissue, with lymph vessels that drain toward lymph nodes in the underarm.

Breast cancer starts when cells in this tissue change and multiply without normal control. Most breast cancers begin in the ducts or the lobules. Over time the cells can form a lump and may spread to nearby lymph nodes or other parts of the body.

It is not one disease. Types, stages and treatments differ widely, which is why every diagnosis is individual. Breast cancer mainly affects women but can also affect men.

WHO also reports that breast cancer occurs in every country, and that outcomes depend heavily on early detection and access to timely, quality treatment. Statistics are updated regularly, so check the current WHO fact sheet for the latest figures.

Possible warning signs

A new lump or thickening

In the breast or underarm, different from the surrounding tissue.

Change in size or shape

One breast noticeably changing in size, shape or contour.

Skin changes

Dimpling, puckering, redness, scaling or an orange-peel texture.

Nipple changes

A nipple turning inward, skin changes, or unusual discharge.

Persistent, unexplained change

Any change that does not settle or keeps returning.

Early detection

Finding breast cancer at an earlier stage often gives more treatment options. It cannot guarantee an outcome, and no single schedule suits everyone.

  1. Know your normalPay attention to how your breasts look and feel over time.
  2. Talk to a professional about changesDo not wait for a new or unusual change to go away. Ask a clinician to assess it.
  3. Ask about screeningScreening, such as mammography, looks for cancer in people without symptoms. Whether and when to start depends on your age, personal and family history, and the guidance where you live.
  4. Know that guidance variesRecommendations differ between countries and health systems. Your doctor or national health authority can tell you what applies to you.

Screening is different from diagnostic testing. Diagnostic tests investigate a symptom or an unusual screening result.

Support for patients, survivors and families

If you are affected

You do not have to cope alone. Ask your care team about counselling, peer support and practical help.

If you love someone affected

Listen without rushing to fix. Offer specific help, such as a ride or a meal, and follow their lead.

If you want to help

Share information from reputable sources, and choose organisations that are open about who they are and how they work.
